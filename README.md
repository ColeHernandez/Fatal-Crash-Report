Can Drunk Driving be Reduced? Saving Lives One Law at a Time


Lives are too precious to be lost at the wheel from either your own decisions or the poor decisions from someone else on the road. The Utah state legislature has recognized this, and they have made an attempt to put a stop to drunk driving. This attempt is by lowering the legal blood alcohol concentration from a .08 to the new .05. This change in BAC has hopefully deterred more drivers with just one or two drinks in them to stay off the road. The question is however, how well did this law work. 
To analyze the hypothesis, has Utah’s alcohol related fatality rate declined meaningfully after 2018 compared to control states that did not change their BAC laws,  we must look at drunk driving data before the law compared to after the law. The data we are using specifically is from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and is a national census of drunk driving related fatalities in each state. However, the data only spans to the year 2020, and the law took place in 2018. This is the only limitation to this experiment, however, we will be able to see a trend nonetheless. 
So in the NHTSA public database, we pulled the FARS (Fatality Analysis Reporting System)  in order to gather all necessary data on fatality related crashes. This data set is not a sample, it is a complete census of all fatalities related to drunk driving, which will allow for the most accurate experiment. The span of data we are using is from 2014 to 2020, where 5 years of data is pre-law and 2 is post-law. 
In order to gather an accurate understanding of how well this law worked, incorporating controls is absolutely necessary. The controls in question are the states: Idaho, Colorado, Wyoming, Florida, as well as the national average. I used the states Idaho, Colorado, and Wyoming as controls due to the fact that they have similar demographics to Utah. This way our experiment is not skewed by states with completely different lifestyles. However, completely disregarding differing states is not a viable way of conducting an experiment, so Florida and California are used as outliers in the experiment. 
To extract this data, the FARS zip archives were downloaded and extracted locally. They were then uploaded to google drive so Google Colab could access them via the command, “from google.colab import drive”. Each archive contains multiple CSVs; only ACCIDENT.csv was used which records one row per fatal crash. Files loaded year by year in a for-loop, then concatenated into a single dataframe, and a YEAR column was added to each. Data was filtered to the six states of interest using their corresponding state codes. Crashes were then flagged as alcohol related by filtering all Drunk_dr columns that had a value greater than zero. Rows missing values in Drunk_Dr or FATALS were dropped. 
Rates were then calculated for each state. This was done via the equation, (alcohol fatalities / total fatalities) * 100. 
Once data was cleaned and ready for experimentation to start, visualizations were used for the main explanatory piece of evidence. To start, a line chart was created. This line chart displayed alcohol fatality rate over time for all 6 states as well as the national average, with a vertical line at 2018 to display when the law was in effect. This chart was chosen because it is the best way to show trends over time and visually demonstrate the pre and post contrast. Especially when incorporating other states into the mix. This chart shows that Utah’s rate visibly declined after 2018 while most control states remained flat or even increased. This rapid decline suggests that the policy had an effect on Utah’s fatality rate. 


	Next the bar chart is used to show average pre law vs post law fatality rate side by side for each state. This simplifies the time series into a direct before and after comparison that is easy to interpret. This bar chart also shows the most pronounced drop among all states being Utah, while all other states showed little to no decline. 


Finally, a heat map is used to show a full matrix of alcohol fatality rates by state and year, color coded from low to high. This visualization is used because it allows simultaneous inspection of all states across all years in a simple numerical view. Again this chart shows that Utah’s post 2018 cells are noticeably greener (lower) than all other states. 

Visually there is an obvious difference between states, however, to confirm this conclusion a couple tests will be used. More specifically the Difference in Difference regression model is used first. This test is modeled using Rate ~ treated + post_law + treated:post_law using Utah as the treated unit and all other states as the control. This regression model is used because it controls for time trends shared by states, isolating the law's specific effect on Utah. It is a more rigorous test than a t - test because it accounts for what would have happened to Utah without the law. This test shows that the treated:post_law coefficient was -9.64 with a p value of .05, meaning Utah’s alcohol fatality rate dropped 9.64 percentage points more that Idaho’s after the law with a statistically significant p value. This is analysis is very convincing of how well this law actually worked, however, more test are needed to gain a proper analysis. 


	The final test will be the t-test due to the fact that its simple and interpretable. This test also allows us to compare Utah’s isolated data from pre law mean to post law mean directly. This test shows a 3.83 percentage point drop, however it did not reach statistical significance. Where t=1.338 and p=.239. This high p-value is most likely attributed to the data only having 2 post law years. So this test lacks sufficient evidence to make a conclusion on the effectiveness of the law. 

	This is where the data’s limitations come into play. Where yes there might be a significant drop in Utah’s drunk driving fatality rate however, with the regression analysis yielding a .05 p-value and the t-test yielding a .239 p-value, we can’t necessarily say our finding is conclusive. The most obvious limiting factor is only having a 2 year post law range. However, there is one major factor at hand, the year of 2019 is also the start of the Covid-19 pandemic. So the following two years after 2018 may have differing results state to state according to pandemic laws and order in place. This may be the reason we see a major jump in Idaho and a drop in Utah. In the future it would be interesting to run this experiment again once more data has been released in order to make a strong conclusion. Although, from this experiment alone, it is obvious that all other states did not have drastic decreases in drunk driving fatality rates, other than Utah. That fact alone can help us make a conclusion with a lower confidence interval, that the law did in fact work.
